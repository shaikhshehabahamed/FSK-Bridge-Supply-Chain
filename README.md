# Pricing the Resilience of Automotive Supply Chains: Lessons from the Francis Scott Key Bridge Collapse

On March 26, 2024, the Francis Scott Key Bridge collapsed and closed the main shipping channel into the Port of Baltimore, the nation's busiest vehicle-import gateway, for 76 days. This paper asks whether the closure broke the automotive supply chains that depend on this gateway or whether the industry absorbed it, and at what cost. This study uses a 72-month panel of national production, parts pricing, and import data. Interrupted time-series models find no statistically distinguishable break in production or pricing once the analysis corrects for multiple testing. This result indicates absorption, not breakage. A new production-continuity method recovers an implied safety-stock buffer of 17.6 days of supply from the April 2024 production shortfall, a figure grounded in what the industry actually held, not stated policy. Vehicle-carrying traffic diverted from Baltimore to Brunswick, Georgia, within months. Pricing the safety-stock buffer against the loss it prevented gives an annual carrying cost of $0.96 billion against a one-time avoided production loss of $2.81 billion. The break-even period is 2.9 years for a disruption of this scale. The total economic cost of the shortfall, including lost wages, ranges from $9.6 billion to $13.6 billion. These results show that the industry can absorb a short, foreseeable closure at a concentrated chokepoint without a detectable break in national output, at a moderate cost. The method introduced here offers a way to price concentration risk at any single-source chokepoint before a closure forces the question.
